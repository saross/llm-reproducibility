# Prior art: how FAIR and reproducibility-assessment instruments handle third-party/reused digital artefacts

## 1. Executive summary

This is a **partially-solved problem with one strong, directly-applicable precedent** and a great deal of instructive silence. Every object-scoped FAIR assessment instrument examined (RDA FAIR Data Maturity Model, F-UJI, FAIRshake, FAIR-Checker, FAIR4RS/FAIRsoft) is architecturally incapable of discriminating by authorship, because their unit of assessment is the digital object itself, identified by its own persistent identifier — "who created it" simply never enters the indicator set. Self-assessment/awareness tools (FAIR-Aware, ARDC's tools) are explicitly framed as pre-deposit, first-person exercises ("your dataset, before depositing") and were never designed to retrospectively audit third-party inputs at all. Paper-scoped badging and audit schemes (ACM Artifact Review and Badging, CODECHECK, COS/*Psychological Science* Open Data and Open Materials badges) are, on close reading of their actual operative criteria, **status-based, not origin-based**: they ask whether the input is permanently, publicly, and citably available — not who put it there — despite looser popular paraphrases (including ACM's own marketing language) sometimes implying "author-created." Large-scale meta-research audits of data/code availability (Stodden 2018, Colavizza 2020, Culina 2020) code availability as a binary/ordinal access-method variable and do not implement any category for reused/secondary data — the silence is by omission, not by considered policy, as far as retrievable text shows. The one clear exception — and the closest direct precedent to the registrant's problem — is the **Journal of Industrial Ecology's Data Openness Badges**, which explicitly separate "foreground" (author-generated) from "background" (third-party/secondary, e.g. ecoinvent) data and award full badge credit when background data is precisely cited/linked rather than re-deposited or re-authored. No instrument found imposes a *penalty* specifically for blameless reliance on well-archived third-party data, and none found requires authorship as a precondition for full marks on an artefact's own FAIRness — which is consistent with, though does not itself dictate, the registrant's working position.

## 3. Per-item findings (a)–(d)

### 1. RDA FAIR Data Maturity Model (2020)
- **(a) Unit of assessment:** A single digital object ("resource"), defined in the glossary as "a resource consisting of units of information... being the primary subject of the FAIR evaluation." Not a paper, not a repository, not a researcher.
- **(b) Third-party/reused inputs in scope?** Implicitly always in scope, because "resource" and "reuse" are defined identically for any object regardless of provenance: "Reuse: The act of using an existing resource for a different purpose or in a different context." There is no indicator that asks who deposited the object.
- **(c) Authorship/responsibility affects scoring?** No. I read all 41 indicators (F1–R1.3) plus priorities, evaluation methods, and the full glossary directly from the primary specification PDF (DOI 10.15497/rda00045). None references creator identity, depositor identity, or "own vs third-party" data. R1.2 ("(meta)data are associated with detailed provenance") is about whether the *object's metadata documents its origin/history* — a well-documented third-party Archaeological Data Service (ADS, UK) deposit can score at the top of R1.2 precisely because someone else did the archival work properly.
- **(d) Citation:** RDA FAIR Data Maturity Model Working Group (2020). *FAIR Data Maturity Model: Specification and Guidelines*. Research Data Alliance. DOI: [10.15497/rda00045](https://doi.org/10.15497/rda00045). CC BY 4.0.

### 2. FAIRsFAIR / F-UJI
- **(a)** A single dataset, addressed by its own persistent identifier (DOI, handle, etc.) — "the dataset level" per its own documentation.
- **(b)** In scope by construction: F-UJI takes a PID as sole input and queries external registries (re3data, DataCite, FAIRsharing) about *that PID's* metadata. It has no concept of "the paper this dataset supports" and therefore cannot see, let alone flag, that the dataset predates or is external to the citing paper.
- **(c)** No. Authorship never appears as a parameter or output field.
- **(d)** Devaraju, A. & Huber, R. *F-UJI – An Automated FAIR Data Assessment Tool*, FAIRsFAIR project. https://www.f-uji.net/ ; code: https://github.com/pangaea-data-publisher/fuji (MIT licence, 79 stars, last push 2026-08-01).

### 3. FAIRshake
- **(a)** "Any digital object" registered in its database (dataset, API, tool, standard).
- **(b)** In scope — the toolkit's data model is `{users, projects, digital resources, metrics, rubrics, assessments}`; a resource can be assessed under any project regardless of who registered it.
- **(c)** No authorship/ownership field found in the described data model.
- **(d)** Clarke, D.J.B. et al. (2019). "FAIRshake: Toolkit to Evaluate the FAIRness of Research Digital Resources." *bioRxiv* 657676; tool: https://fairshake.cloud/ ; code: https://github.com/MaayanLab/FAIRshake (10 stars, last push 2025-08-28, licence field returns NOASSERTION on GitHub — worth checking manually before relying on licence compatibility).

### 4. FAIR-Aware (DANS)
- **(a)** A dataset — but framed strictly as "your" dataset.
- **(b)** Out of scope by design intent: the tool's own framing is "You can use this tool at any point during your research **before depositing** your data(set)." It is a prospective, first-person awareness exercise for the data creator, not a retrospective third-party audit instrument. Applying it to someone else's already-deposited ADS record would be a misuse of the tool's stated purpose.
- **(c)** Not applicable in the sense asked — the tool assumes the user *is* the depositor; it has no mechanism for a third party (e.g. our registrant, scoring published papers) to assess someone else's dataset.
- **(d)** DANS/FAIRsFAIR. *FAIR-Aware*. https://fairaware.dans.knaw.nl/ ; predecessor SATIFYD tool description: Zenodo record 10809544.

### 5. ARDC FAIR Data Self-Assessment Tool
- **(a)** A dataset ("your research dataset").
- **(b)/(c)** Same structural position as FAIR-Aware — a first-person, pre/post-deposit self-improvement checklist across four F/A/I/R sections, not a third-party retrospective audit tool. No provision for assessing an artefact one did not create.
- **(d)** Australian Research Data Commons. *FAIR Data Self-Assessment Tool*. https://ardc.edu.au/resource/fair-data-self-assessment-tool/

### 6. fair-software.eu / howfairis
- **(a)** A software repository as a whole (the unit `howfairis` operates on is a GitHub/GitLab repo URL).
- **(b)** In scope in the sense that the whole repository — including whatever third-party dependencies it vendors or references — is what gets the five-point score, but the five recommendations (public repository, licence, registry, citation, quality checklist) are properties of the *repository itself*, not of any dependency. Fetched text of Recommendation 1 confirms it "makes no distinction between original code and other components" and contains no mention of third-party dependencies at all.
- **(c)** No.
- **(d)** Netherlands eScience Center & DANS. *fair-software.eu*. https://fair-software.eu/ ; tool: https://github.com/fair-software/howfairis (Apache-2.0, 74 stars, last push 2025-05-07).

### 7. FAIR4RS Principles and instruments built on them
- **(a)** A software artefact (the "research software" object), analogous to F-UJI's dataset-object framing.
- **(b)** Dependencies are explicitly addressed but under a *different* question than authorship: FAIR4RS calls for "qualified references to external dependencies... via the qualifying authority" (package managers: CRAN, PyPI, npm, Maven) so that the *software's own metadata* correctly cites what it needs to run. This is a documentation-completeness signal (does the software properly declare its CRAN/PyPI dependencies?), not a rule for scoring whether those dependencies are themselves FAIR, nor a penalty for depending on artefacts the authors didn't write. FAIRsoft (the OpenEBench/ELIXIR practical implementation) scores individual software objects against these indicators; it does not roll up or discount for unauthored dependencies.
- **(c)** No — dependency citation quality is scored as a property of the described object's metadata, not of who wrote the dependency.
- **(d)** Barker, M. et al. (2022). "Introducing the FAIR Principles for research software." *Scientific Data* 9, 622. DOI: [10.1038/s41597-022-01710-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-022-01710-x). RDA Recommendation: DOI 10.15497/RDA00068. FAIRsoft: Bermudez-Losa et al., described at https://openebench.bsc.es/observatory/Evaluation/ (unable to verify exact FAIRsoft journal DOI this session — do not cite it without independent confirmation).

### 8. FAIR-Checker
- **(a)** A "digital resource" identified by PID or landing-page URL/DOI — dataset-object level, same family as F-UJI.
- **(b)** In scope by the same mechanism as F-UJI: the user supplies a PID/URL and the tool queries that resource's own metadata via SPARQL/SHACL against Semantic Web standards, indifferent to depositor.
- **(c)** No.
- **(d)** Gaignard, A. et al. "FAIR-Checker: supporting digital resource findability and reuse with Knowledge Graphs and Semantic Web standards." PMC10315041; code: https://github.com/IFB-ElixirFr/FAIR-checker (MIT, 29 stars, last push 2026-08-05).

### 9. ACM Artifact Review and Badging (v1.1)
- **(a)** The **paper** is the nominal unit ("artifacts associated with the paper"), but the badge itself attaches to whatever artefact bundle the authors submit for review.
- **(b)** In scope, and this is the most interesting case of a stated-vs-operative gap found in this search. Secondary summaries (including ACM's own explanatory prose in some venue-specific call-for-artifacts pages, e.g. SIGSAC) gloss the "Artifacts Available" badge as requiring **"author-created artifacts"** be placed in an archival repository. But the actual current (v1.1, in force since 2020-08-24) badge *definition* text — confirmed by direct fetch of an ACM-affiliated call-for-artifacts page quoting the policy verbatim — reads only: **"the artifacts associated with the paper have been made available for retrieval, permanently and publicly."** That sentence contains no authorship gate. It is entirely plausible under the letter of v1.1 for a paper whose "artifact" is essentially a wrapper around a third-party dataset/tool to receive "Artifacts Available," provided the wrapper (and, ideally, a resolvable pointer to the third-party material) is itself permanently archived. In practice, individual venues' artifact-evaluation committees (CCS, SIGIR, EuroSys, etc.) may apply the stricter "author-created" gloss at their discretion — this varies by venue and was not centrally reconciled by ACM in the material I could retrieve.
- **(c)** Formally, no (per the badge-definition sentence); informally/by convention at some venues, yes (per the "author-created" paraphrase some committees use). This ambiguity is itself a directly relevant finding for the registrant: even a widely-cited badging scheme has drifted into inconsistent practice on exactly this question.
- **(d)** ACM. *Artifact Review and Badging – Version 1.1 (Current)*. https://www.acm.org/publications/policies/artifact-review-and-badging-current

### 10. CODECHECK
- **(a)** A single paper's computational claim(s) — "computations underlying a research article."
- **(b)** Explicitly and deliberately in scope, and CODECHECK is the cleanest "status not origin" example found. Its author-workflow guide states: data "may be deposited depending on community practices in remote repositories, synthetic data may be used, subsets or preprocessed data may be included." A codecheck can and does proceed against third-party input data with no bearing on the certificate awarded — the check is purely "given the declared inputs (wherever sourced) and declared code, do the declared outputs reproduce?"
- **(c)** No — CODECHECK's five principles ("codecheckers record but don't investigate or fix," etc.) are about the *checker's* conduct, not about who produced the inputs being checked.
- **(d)** Nüst, D. et al. (2021). "CODECHECK: an Open Science initiative for the independent execution of computations underlying research articles during peer review to improve reproducibility." *F1000Research* 10:253. PMC8311796. Project: https://codecheck.org.uk/ ; example tooling: https://github.com/codecheckers/codecheck (MIT, 11 stars, last push 2026-08-05).

### 11. Center for Open Science / *Psychological Science* Open Data & Open Materials badges
- **(a)** The paper (badge is awarded to, and displayed alongside, the published article).
- **(b)** In scope by the operative criterion text located: the Open Data badge requires "all digitally shareable data relevant to the publication" (or, per COS's own phrasing, "all data necessary to reproduce the reported results") be deposited in an open repository with a persistent identifier — again an access-method/permanence test, not an origin test. I could **not** locate a definitive, quotable FAQ entry resolving the specific secondary-data-analysis case (does a paper reanalysing someone else's already-deposited public dataset qualify?) despite multiple targeted searches of the COS/OSF FAQ pages and the `open_data_badge_criteria.txt` file (fetch attempts returned only page chrome, not body text) — flagged as an unresolved gap rather than a negative finding. Indirect evidence (the Journal of Industrial Ecology's badge variant, item 18, built in the same "badge" tradition) suggests the broader badge family is comfortable crediting linked third-party data, but this is inference from an adjacent scheme, not confirmation of the core COS text.
- **(c)** No explicit authorship gate found in the retrievable criteria text; "available upon reasonable request" is explicitly disqualified regardless of who would be asked.
- **(d)** Kidwell, M.C. et al. (2016). "Badges to Acknowledge Open Practices: A Simple, Low-Cost, Effective Method for Increasing Transparency." *PLOS Biology* 14(5): e1002456. DOI: [10.1371/journal.pbio.1002456](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1002456). Badge criteria: https://www.cos.io/initiatives/badges

### 12. CoreTrustSeal (repository-level contrast case)
- **(a)** The **repository**, not a dataset and not a paper — requested specifically for contrast, and it delivers a genuinely different axis of assessment. All 16 numbered requirements (R01–R16, organisational/digital-object-management/technical-infrastructure groups) are "equally weighted, standalone items" scored once for the whole institution.
- **(b)** Third-party-deposited data is not merely "in scope" — it is the entire *raison d'être* of the requirements: a repository by definition holds material it did not create. R07 ("Data Integrity and Authenticity") requires the repository to document "the degree of reliability of the original deposited data and its provenance"; R08 covers the deposit/appraisal workflow; R02/R04 (Rights Management; Legal & Ethical) require the repository to hold or obtain the rights needed to preserve/share data it received from depositors.
- **(c)** Responsibility is explicitly reallocated, not erased: the *repository* (not the original data creator) is accountable for integrity, rights management, and appraisal of what it holds, regardless of who deposited it. This is a structurally different answer to "does authorship matter" than any dataset- or paper-level instrument gives — worth noting in the registrant's methods section as the clearest illustration that "unit of assessment" and "locus of responsibility" are two independently adjustable dials.
- **(d)** CoreTrustSeal Standards and Certification Board. *CoreTrustSeal Trustworthy Data Repositories Requirements 2023–2025*. Zenodo: https://zenodo.org/records/7051096 ; overview: https://www.coretrustseal.org/why-certification/requirements/

### 13. Stodden, Seiler & Ma (2018), PNAS
- **(a)** The paper (per-article postpublication remission of "raw data... code... workflows... input parameters").
- **(b)** In scope in the sense that any artefact the authors "make available" upon request counts toward the reported (low) reproduction rate; no distinction located between artefacts the authors generated versus artefacts they merely used and cite from elsewhere.
- **(c)** No authorship/responsibility distinction located in the retrievable abstract/summary material.
- **(d)** Stodden, V., Seiler, J. & Ma, Z. (2018). "An empirical analysis of journal policy effectiveness for computational reproducibility." *PNAS* 115(11), 2584–2589. DOI: [10.1073/pnas.1708290115](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1708290115)

### 14. Colavizza, Hrynaszkiewicz, Staden, Whitaker & McGillivray (2020), PLOS ONE
- **(a)** The paper (each article's Data Availability Statement is the unit coded).
- **(b)** In scope, and I obtained the actual four-category coding scheme via direct fetch of the PMC full text: (0) not available; (1) available on request; (2) available with the paper/supplementary files; (3) available in a repository, via URL or (preferably) a persistent identifier. **Category 3 does not distinguish a newly-minted author deposit from a pointer to a pre-existing third-party repository entry** — both a paper depositing its own new dataset and a paper merely citing someone else's decade-old DOI would code identically as "3."
- **(c)** No — the coding scheme is purely about access method, confirmed directly from the paper's own methodology as summarised in the retrieved text.
- **(d)** Colavizza, G., Hrynaszkiewicz, I., Staden, I., Whitaker, K. & McGillivray, B. (2020). "The citation advantage of linking publications to research data." *PLOS ONE* 15(4): e0230416. DOI: [10.1371/journal.pone.0230416](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0230416)

### 15. Culina, van den Berg, Evans & Sánchez-Tójar (2020), PLOS Biology
- **(a)** The paper (is analytical code shared, yes/no, across a sample of ecology articles).
- **(b)** In scope, broadly defined (even screenshot-based protocols count as "code" if they'd let someone reproduce the quantitative results) — but explicitly, per the retrieved full text, **the paper "does not address whether code merely calling third-party packages counts toward availability"** and draws **no distinction between author-written analysis code and reused/dependency code**. - **(c)** No.

### 18. *Journal of Industrial Ecology* Data Openness Badges — closest direct precedent
- **(a)** The paper (badge awarded per published article), but scored via a **two-part decomposition** of the paper's data into foreground and background components — itself a noteworthy structural move not seen in any other scheme reviewed.
- **(b)** Explicitly and precisely in scope, with a worked rule: an LCA (life-cycle assessment) study "makes available its foreground (all process descriptions based on own research and primary data) and also publishes all the links to a published data set (e.g., ecoinvent) for all secondary data used." For proprietary/licensed secondary databases, the requirement becomes precise citation — "naming the exact database version (e.g., ecoinvent v3.5, cut-off allocation) and all referenced processes" with identifiers — rather than re-deposit (which would often be impossible or unlawful for licensed third-party databases). The badge's own framing states the critical requirement is "transparency and reproducibility, not data ownership."
- **(c)** Deliberately decoupled: authorship/ownership of secondary data is irrelevant to badge eligibility provided it is precisely and citably referenced; only the *foreground* (author-generated) component is held to a full-deposit standard.
- **(d)** *Journal of Industrial Ecology* Editorial Board, following Hertwich, E.G. et al. (2018) policy note (exact DOI not independently confirmed this session — cite the live policy page pending verification). Policy page: https://jie.yale.edu/data-openness-badges

### 19. COPE — "Authorship when data sets are reused as secondary data"
- **(a)** Not a FAIR-scoring unit at all — this is publication-ethics case guidance about co-authorship entitlement.
- **(b)** Addresses a genuinely different question (does the *original data collector* deserve co-authorship on the *secondary* paper?), not whether the secondary paper's FAIRness score should be affected by using someone else's data.
- **(c)** Yes, but on the authorship-credit axis, not the FAIRness axis — useful precisely because it shows the wider research-integrity ecosystem keeps these two questions ("who deserves credit" vs "is this artefact/paper FAIR") formally separate, which supports treating them separately in the registrant's instrument too.
- **(d)** Committee on Publication Ethics. "Authorship when data sets are reused as secondary data." https://publicationethics.org/guidance/case/authorship-when-data-sets-are-reused-secondary-data

## 4. Synthesis: the spectrum of approaches

Two independent axes emerged, and every instrument reviewed can be placed on both.

**Axis 1 — Unit of assessment (object-scoped ↔ paper-scoped ↔ repository-scoped):**
- *Object-scoped* (RDA FDMM, F-UJI, FAIRshake, FAIR-Checker, FAIR4RS/FAIRsoft): the thing being scored is the artefact itself, addressed by its own identifier. These instruments are **structurally blind** to authorship — not through a considered exemption, but because their data model has no slot for "who made this." A third-party ADS deposit and an author's own freshly-minted Zenodo deposit are indistinguishable inputs to F-UJI; both get scored purely on their own metadata/PID/licence properties.
- *Paper-scoped* (ACM badging, CODECHECK, COS/*Psychological Science* badges, Stodden/Colavizza/Culina/Marwick/Tedersoo audits): the thing being scored is the paper's practice — did it make its inputs available? Here authorship *could* matter (the paper's authors are a natural locus of responsibility), yet in every operative-criterion text I could actually quote, the test applied is **status** ("is it there, permanently, publicly, citably, in usable form?"), not **origin** ("did you make it?"). CODECHECK states this most explicitly by name-checking "remote repositories" as an acceptable data source.
- *Repository-scoped* (CoreTrustSeal): the thing being scored is neither the artefact nor the paper but the institution stewarding many artefacts of necessarily mixed provenance. Responsibility is assigned to the repository as custodian, which is the one place authorship/provenance is a first-class, scored concept — but it is the *repository's* documentation of provenance that's scored, not the original creator's identity or the citing paper's.
- *Self-assessment/awareness tools* (FAIR-Aware, ARDC tools) sit outside this axis entirely: they assume the assessor *is* the creator/depositor and are not built to answer the registrant's question at all.

**Axis 2 — Status-based vs responsibility-based scoring:**
Almost everything found is status-based: does the artefact/paper currently meet the criterion, full stop, regardless of causal history. The only instrument found that builds an explicit **responsibility split** into its scoring is the *Journal of Industrial Ecology*'s foreground/background badge, which asks a fully-original question the registrant is also asking: "should the fully-open-and-shared parts of a paper get different treatment from the necessarily-external parts?" — and answers it by decoupling deposit-obligation (foreground only) from citation-obligation (background/secondary, always required, satisfiable by precise linking rather than re-authoring or re-hosting). CoreTrustSeal's R07/R08 come closest to a genuine *responsibility* (not merely status) test, but at the repository level, not the paper or object level, so it doesn't transfer directly to a paper-scoring instrument.

No instrument reviewed **penalises** a paper/object for depending on a third-party artefact that is itself well-archived; several (F-UJI, RDA FDMM, JIE badges) can award **full credit** for a well-archived third-party input precisely because the instrument's unit of assessment is the input itself, not its creator. Equally, no instrument reviewed formalises the registrant's flip side — explicitly penalising closed/unpublished third-party inputs "even when blameless" — as a *named, considered* policy; the closest analogues (Colavizza's Category 0/1 "not available"/"on request," CODECHECK's implicit requirement that declared inputs actually be resolvable) achieve the same practical effect by omission (an unavailable input simply fails the status test, whoever is at fault) rather than by an articulated "blameless third-party unavailability still counts against the paper" rule.

## 5. Recommendations

- **Use directly as documented justification:** The *Journal of Industrial Ecology* Data Openness Badges (item 18) foreground/background language is close enough to the registrant's working position that it is worth quoting (with citation) in the instrument's methods/limitations section as an existing precedent for "credit third-party inputs when precisely cited, regardless of who created them."
- **Cite as structural precedent for object-blindness-to-authorship:** RDA FAIR Data Maturity Model (item 1) and its glossary definition of "resource"/"reuse" is the cleanest, most authoritative textual evidence that the dominant FAIR-assessment paradigm simply does not encode authorship — useful to cite when defending the registrant's position against a reviewer who assumes FAIR assessment is inherently author-attributive.
- **Cite as the "status not origin" precedent for the code side:** CODECHECK's explicit acceptance of third-party/remote/synthetic input data (item 10) and Culina et al.'s (item 15) explicit non-distinction between author-written and dependency-calling code are the two strongest analogues for the registrant's CRAN-package example.
- **Adapt approach, not implementation:** CoreTrustSeal's repository-as-locus-of-responsibility model (item 12) is not directly portable (wrong unit of assessment) but is worth one sentence in the methods section as the deliberate contrast case the user requested — it demonstrates that "does authorship matter" is answerable differently depending on what you choose as the unit of assessment, which is exactly the degree of freedom the registrant is exercising.
- **Note the ACM inconsistency as a cautionary example, not a model to follow:** ACM's badge-text/popular-paraphrase gap (item 9) is worth flagging in the registrant's documentation as evidence that *even well-established schemes drift into ambiguity here* — an argument for the registrant to be more explicit and precise than ACM has been, not for adopting ACM's approach.
- **Ignore as non-transferable:** FAIR-Aware and the ARDC self-assessment tool (items 4–5) — these are pre-deposit, first-person tools with no retrospective third-party-audit capability; they answer a different question from the outset and citing them as precedent would be a category error.
- **Flag as unresolved, worth a direct follow-up read:** Marwick (2017) and Tedersoo et al. (2021) (items 16–17) — genuine full-text access failures this session, not negative findings. Given Marwick's direct relevance to archaeology and the FAIMS (Field Acquired Information Management Systems)/Fieldmark connection, this one is worth 10 minutes of Shawn's own institutional access rather than further scout effort.

## 6. Build-vs-adopt verdict

This is not a software-adoption question, so "build vs adopt" translates to: **adopt existing conceptual precedent, then build the registrant's own explicit rule, because no instrument reviewed states the registrant's exact rule as a named, general-purpose policy.**

1. **Adopt the JIE foreground/background framing as citable precedent** for the principle "credit well-archived third-party inputs in full; the deposit obligation attaches only to what the authors themselves generated." This gives the registrant's working position an existing, peer-reviewed-adjacent analogue rather than requiring it to be presented as a novel policy invented for this study.
2. **Adopt the RDA FDMM's object-definition logic** as the structural justification for *why* status-based (not origin-based) scoring is the norm across the field's most authoritative instrument, strengthening the methodological defence against a reviewer who might otherwise expect authorship-sensitive scoring by default.
3. **Build a short, explicit rule of the registrant's own**, because none of the twelve requested frameworks — nor the five meta-research audits, nor the two badge/ethics schemes found opportunistically — states, in so many words, "score the empirical FAIRness status of the research as published, regardless of artefact ownership or fault, with full credit for well-archived third-party inputs and penalty for closed inputs even when blameless." The closest approaches (JIE, RDA FDMM, CODECHECK) support this position by structural analogy and by omission-of-contrary-rule, but the registrant will be the first to state it as a named principle for a 15-sub-principle census instrument scoring published papers rather than depositing repositories or software objects directly. This should be written up as a short methods-section paragraph citing items 1, 10, 15, and 18 as precedent, explicitly acknowledging (per Section 4) that the instrument is thereby choosing paper-scoped-but-status-based scoring, consistent with the dominant pattern in the field rather than a departure from it.
4. **No combination of existing tools is needed** — this is a documentation/justification task for an already-designed instrument, not a tool-integration task.
